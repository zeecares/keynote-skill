# The Winston rules

Distilled from Patrick Winston's "How to Speak" (MIT OpenCourseWare,
RES.TLL-005, January IAP 2018; official transcript published with MIT
15.371 Innovation Teams, Fall 2024). Quotes below are verbatim from that
transcript; timestamps follow the OCW lecture outline.

Source: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/pages/how-to-speak/
Transcript: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-371-innovation-teams-fall-2024/resources/unzc731icuy_transcript/

## 0. Why it matters (Introduction @00:16)

"Your success in life will be determined largely by your ability to speak,
your ability to write, and the quality of your ideas, in that order."

Communication quality = knowledge x practice x talent, "and notice that the
T is very small. What really matters is what you know." The rules are
learnable; that is the whole point of encoding them here.

## 1. How to start (@04:15)

- **Do not open with a joke.** "In the beginning of a talk, people are still
  putting their laptops away... they're not ready for a joke. So it doesn't
  work very well."
- **Open with an empowerment promise.** "You want to tell people what they're
  going to know at the end of the hour that they didn't know at the beginning
  of the hour."

Deck rule: slide 2 states the promise in one sentence. No joke on slide 1.

## 2. Four sample heuristics (@05:38)

- **Cycle on the subject.** Go around the big idea three times. "At any given
  moment, about 20% of you will be fogged out no matter what the lecture is."
- **Build a fence around your idea.** Distinguish it from every idea it could
  be confused with: "my algorithm might seem similar to Jones's algorithm,
  except his is exponential, and mine's linear."
- **Verbal punctuation.** Announce seams and landmarks ("the third idea
  is...") so people who fogged out can get back on the bus.
- **Ask a question.** Calibrated: not too obvious, not too hard. You can wait
  about seven seconds for an answer.

Deck rule: one idea per slide; headline carries the idea; section-divider
slides are verbal punctuation.

## 3. Time and place (@10:17)

- Best lecture time: about 11 AM - everyone awake, nobody back asleep.
- **Keep the lights full up.** "It's extremely hard to see slides through
  closed eyelids."
- **Case the room beforehand** so there are no surprises.
- The room should be more than half full.

## 4. Boards, props, slides (@13:24)

- **Boards are for informing/teaching; slides are for exposing ideas** - job
  talks and conference talks expose, they do not teach.
- Boards have graphic quality, the right speed ("the speed with which you
  write on the blackboard is approximately the speed at which people can
  absorb ideas"), and they give your hands a target.
- **Props are what people remember.** (Papert's bicycle wheel; the
  conservation-of-energy pendulum; the broken pointer.) Winston's
  explanation: empathetic mirroring - the audience physically feels the
  board work and the prop; a slide cannot do that.

### The slide crimes

- **Too many words.** The airport diagnosis: "You have too many, and they
  have too many words... it's always true."
- **Reading the slide.** "People in your audience know how to read, and
  reading will just annoy them."
- **One language processor.** "We humans only have one language processor,
  and we can either use it to read stuff or to listen to the speaker." In
  his student's experiment, audiences remembered what they read, not what
  was said - one subject reported "I wish you hadn't talked so much."
- **Fixes, in order:** remove background junk, remove words, remove logos,
  remove the title ("I'm telling you the title. It doesn't have to be up
  there"), eliminate clutter ("No reason even for those bullets").
- **Font floor.** "40 or 50" pt is about right; "35 is beginning to get too
  small" - small fonts exist to cram words, which is the actual crime.
- **No laser pointer.** You cannot use one without turning away from the
  audience: "you lose contact with the audience." Instead, put a little
  arrow on the image and say "look at that guy at the end of arrow number
  one."
- **Not too heavy.** Print the deck and lay it on a table: you want air -
  white space and imagery, not text walls.
- **Hapax legomenon.** One overwhelmingly complex slide per presentation,
  max - and only when incomprehensibility is the point.
- **Slides are condiments, not the main event.** Stand next to the slide,
  not across the room from it (no "tennis match").

## 5. Informing: promise, inspiration, how to think (@36:30)

- Start with the promise, and show something cool early (his map-coloring
  demo: from "the sun will have exploded" to seconds).
- **How people are inspired:** a teacher who told them they could do it;
  someone who helped them see a problem in a new way; and above all,
  someone who "exhibited passion about what they were doing."
- Teaching people to think = giving them stories, the questions to ask about
  those stories, mechanisms for analyzing them, ways of putting them
  together, and ways of judging their reliability.

## 6. Persuading: oral exams, job talks (@41:30)

- Oral-exam failures are usually "failure to situate and a failure to
  practice." Practice with people who DON'T know your work - friends and
  supervisors hallucinate missing material into your talk.
- **Job talks: vision + done-something, inside five minutes.** Vision = a
  problem somebody cares about + something new in your approach.
  Done-something = enumerate the steps needed to solve the problem and say
  which you have done.
- **Conclude by enumerating contributions** - the mirror of the steps.

## 7. Getting famous: Winston's star (@41:30 ff.)

"Your ideas are like your children. And you don't want them to go into the
world in rags."

Five S's that make work remembered (his arch-learning thesis accidentally
had all five):

1. **Symbol** - the arch itself.
2. **Slogan** - "one shot learning."
3. **Surprise** - "you don't need a million examples of something to learn."
4. **Salient idea** - not "important", an idea that sticks out (the near
   miss). "Some theses, funnily enough, have too many good ideas."
5. **Story** - how you did it, how it works, why it matters.

## 8. How to stop (@53:06)

### The final slide

Stays on screen through Q&A and the file-out. Do not waste it:

- Not a giant collaborator list (that is a letdown - collaborators go on the
  FIRST slide).
- Not "Questions?" ("I've seen it up there for 20 minutes").
- Not "Thank you" or a blank/URL slide.
- Not bare "Conclusions" - "these are perfectly legitimate conclusions that
  nobody cares about. What they care about is what you have done."
- **Label it CONTRIBUTIONS.**

### The final words

- A joke now works ("by the time you're done, people have adjusted
  themselves to your voice parameters").
- **Do not end on "thank you."** "It suggests that everybody has stayed that
  long out of politeness." Once applause starts, mouthing thanks is fine.
- Options that work: a benediction, a conventioned ending, or **salute the
  audience** - say what you valued about being there.

## Quick checklist (what the linter enforces)

- [ ] Slide 2 states the empowerment promise
- [ ] <= 30 readable words per slide
- [ ] No inline font below the floor; default body type is large
- [ ] No more than 6 bullets per slide, no nesting deeper than 2
- [ ] Final slide labelled Contributions
- [ ] No "Thank you" / "Questions?" anywhere on the final slide
- [ ] Collaborators on the title slide, not the last
- [ ] Every image has alt text (and an arrow, not a laser pointer, in the talk)
